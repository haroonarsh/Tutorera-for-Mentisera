# IGCSE Maths Revision: How to Build a Topic-by-Topic Study Plan

**Status:** Draft — requires editorial review, citations to current awarding-body materials where relevant, and publication approval.

**Suggested URL:** /blog/igcse-maths-revision

IGCSE Mathematics revision works best when it combines syllabus coverage, practice without notes, careful marking and a realistic timetable. Re-reading notes may feel productive, but it does not show whether a student can solve an unfamiliar question under examination conditions. A revision plan should make weaknesses visible and give students repeated opportunities to correct them.

## Confirm the examination specification first

IGCSE Mathematics is offered by different awarding bodies and may have different syllabus codes, tiers, assessment structures and permitted calculator arrangements. Before planning revision, identify the student's exact awarding body, syllabus code, examination series and entry option. Download the current syllabus and official specimen or past-paper materials from the awarding body's website. Do not assume that a topic list or paper format from another specification applies.

## Build a diagnostic topic map

Create a table with each syllabus topic, a confidence rating and evidence from a short set of questions. Useful broad categories may include number, algebra, geometry, measures, coordinate geometry, statistics and probability, but the exact breakdown must follow the current syllabus. Label topics green only when the student can solve questions independently and explain the method. Mark a topic amber when success depends on hints, and red when a core idea is missing.

## Prioritise foundations that affect other topics

Some skills recur across many questions. Fractions, percentages, ratio, rearranging formulae and interpreting graphs can affect performance well beyond their own chapters. If a student struggles with a complex question, identify the earliest missing step. For example, difficulty solving a simultaneous-equations problem may come from errors with negative numbers rather than from misunderstanding the elimination method.

## Use a repeatable study cycle

A practical session can begin with five minutes of recall from earlier topics, continue with a short worked example, then move to independent practice. Mark answers against reliable solutions and record why errors occurred. End by solving a fresh question without looking at the example. Revisit the topic after a gap rather than assuming one successful session means the skill is secure.

## Keep an error log

Record the date, topic, question source, mistake type and corrected method. Distinguish a conceptual error from arithmetic, misreading, weak notation and time pressure. A note such as “I forgot to reverse the inequality when dividing by a negative number” is actionable. “Careless mistake” is not. Return to the same error type with a different question to test whether the correction has transferred.

## Balance topic practice with mixed questions

Topic-specific exercises help students learn a method. Mixed practice tests whether they can recognise which method is appropriate. Once a student can complete straightforward questions, introduce problems that combine skills and require interpretation. Gradually increase the proportion of mixed and timed practice as the examination approaches.

## Use past papers carefully

Official past papers are valuable for understanding question style, mark allocation and time management. However, completing many papers without analysing mistakes is inefficient. After each paper, review the mark scheme, classify errors and select targeted practice. Keep some papers unseen for later timed assessments. Check the current examination rules and permitted materials for the student's exact specification.

## Create a realistic weekly timetable

A workable schedule might include three short topic sessions, one mixed-practice session and one review session. Students with limited time should focus on high-impact weaknesses and consistent practice rather than an ambitious timetable they cannot maintain. Build in rest, school commitments and other subjects. Adjust the plan each week using evidence from completed work.

## When a tutor can help

A tutor may be useful when the student cannot diagnose an error, repeatedly applies a procedure without understanding it or needs feedback on multi-step solutions. Share the syllabus, recent work and error log before the first lesson. Ask the tutor to demonstrate how they will measure independent progress. Avoid guarantees about examination grades.

## Final revision checklist

Confirm the correct syllabus; map topics; diagnose gaps; practise without notes; mark carefully; maintain an error log; revisit weak skills; complete mixed questions; and rehearse examination timing. A successful revision plan is not the one with the most hours. It is the one that produces reliable evidence of learning.
